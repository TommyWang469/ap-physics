**Model:** A taut, massless string passes over a light, frictionless fixed pulley. The string does not stretch. Air resistance is neglected. The left mass is m_1; the right mass is m_2.

**Main relationship:** Acceleration depends on the mass difference divided by the total mass.

|a| = g |m_2 − m_1| / (m_1 + m_2)

The heavier mass accelerates downward and the lighter mass accelerates upward. This describes acceleration; an initial velocity could point the other way.

## U2-ATWOOD-PRELAB-01 — Equal masses

**Topics:** Newton’s first law; equilibrium; initial conditions

**Question:** If two equal masses are suspended from either end of a string passing over a light pulley (an Atwood’s machine), what kind of motion do you expect to occur? Why?

**Answer:** If released from rest, both masses remain at rest. Their weights are equal, so there is no unbalanced force driving the system and a = 0. The tension balances the weight of each mass: T = mg. If the masses are already moving, they continue at constant speed in this ideal model.

## U2-ATWOOD-PRELAB-02 — Changing the masses

**Topics:** Newton’s second law; mass difference; total mass; controlled variables

**Question:** For an Atwood’s machine, how would you expect the acceleration to change if you (a) Move mass from one side to the other, keeping the total mass constant (b) Gradually increase the mass of both sides?

**(a) Keep total mass constant:** Acceleration magnitude increases when the mass difference increases. Moving mass from the lighter side to the heavier side increases the acceleration. Moving mass from the heavier side to the lighter side decreases the acceleration until the masses are equal. Further transfer reverses which side accelerates downward and increases the magnitude again.

**(b) Add the same amount of mass to each side:** The acceleration magnitude decreases if the masses were unequal. The difference in their weights stays the same, but there is more total mass to accelerate. If the masses were equal, the acceleration stays zero.

After adding Δm to each side:
|a′| = g |m_2 − m_1| / (m_1 + m_2 + 2Δm)

**Wording note:** Part (b) does not specify how mass is added. The answer above assumes equal additions. If both masses are multiplied by the same factor, acceleration is unchanged because the mass difference and total mass scale together. Other unequal additions depend on the resulting ratio.

## U2-ATWOOD-PRELAB-03 — Why the accelerations match

**Topics:** String-length constraint; acceleration vectors

**Question:** Why do the two masses have the same acceleration?

**Answer:** They have the same **acceleration magnitude**, in opposite vertical directions. A taut string of fixed length requires one mass to move up exactly as far as the other moves down. Their changes in speed therefore have equal magnitudes.

Taking downward as positive on both sides:
y_1 + y_2 = constant, so a_1 + a_2 = 0.

Thus a_1 = −a_2. The fixed string length causes this relationship; equal tension alone does not explain it.

## U2-ATWOOD-PRELAB-04 — Free-body diagrams

**Topics:** Object-specific forces; tension; weight; free-body diagrams

**Question:** Draw a free-body diagram of the left side mass. Draw another of the right side mass. Include all forces acting on each mass.

**Answer:** Each hanging mass has exactly two forces: upward tension from the string and downward gravitational force from Earth. The same tension T acts on both masses in this ideal model.

### Free-body diagrams

```text
Left mass m1                Right mass m2
     ↑ F_T = T                  ↑ F_T = T
     ●                          ●
     ↓ F_g = m1g                ↓ F_g = m2g
```

Tension: string on each mass. Weight: Earth on each mass. The PDF illustrates m2 > m1 with equal tension arrows and m1g < T < m2g. No mass values or inequality are specified in the source; this inequality is an illustrative case.

These arrows show forces **on each mass**. Tension points upward because the string pulls each mass toward the pulley. There is no normal force because neither mass rests on a surface. Acceleration is not an extra force.

**Newton’s second law:** For the illustrated case m_2 &gt; m_1, choose upward positive for the left mass and downward positive for the right mass. Both then have the same positive acceleration a.

Left: T − m_1g = m_1a
Right: m_2g − T = m_2a
Add: (m_2 − m_1)g = (m_1 + m_2)a

## Study-guide takeaways

- Zero acceleration can mean rest or constant-velocity motion.
- Compare mass difference with total mass. State what stays constant.
- Connected masses have equal acceleration magnitudes, not equal acceleration vectors.
- Tension and weight are forces on the mass; acceleration is not a force.
- Equal tensions do not imply equal weights or zero acceleration.
